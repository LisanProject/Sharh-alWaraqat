1 Lecture III: Defining Fiqh and Legal Rulings Continued [12/06/2025, Jumada al-Thani 15, 1447 AH]

Never Too Late to Pivot in Seeking Knowledge

It is narrated that Imam Malik ibn Anas in his youth was inclined toward singing and leisure. His mother, recognizing his intelligence, dressed him in the clothing of students of knowledge, placed a turban on his head, and said to him: “Go to the scholars, learn from their manners before you learn from their knowledge.” By her encouragement and his sincerity, Imam Malik left what distracted him and devoted himself to sacred learning, eventually becoming one of the greatest scholars of Islam.

This lesson continues our study of al-Waraqat with Sheikh Abdullahi by clarifying the usuli meaning of legal rulings (ahkam), distinguishing obligation from action, and explaining how rulings relate to the acts of the mukallaf. The focus of the lesson is on recapping the previous Ahkam, while going into detail on karaha, and tahrim. Sheikh Abdullahi goes into detail on three different case studies and making sense of the ruling types within their proper contexts.

1.2 Recap

The lecture begins with a brief recap of Lectures I and II by defining ahkam (legal rulings). Sheikh Abdullahi brings up an interesting point that is a point of contention and debate amongst the scholars.

The issue raised in this lecture is the debate over whether a Prophet can legislate independently. Sheikh Abdullahi hinted that the Shari’ (legislator) lexicon is used interchangeably between Allah (when speaking about the Qur’an) and the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ (when speaking about the Hadith literature) and that we’ll discuss this in more detail when we speak about Ijtihad.

Abdullah ibn Masud on Divine Address

Abdullah ibn Masud رضي الله عنه said in meaning:

Whenever you hear Allah say, O you who believe, then listen carefully. It is either a command you are being ordered to carry out or a prohibition you are being forbidden from.

1.3 Iqtida, Jazim, and Ghayr Jazim in the Shafii Madhhab

In the Shafii school, legal rulings are analyzed by examining the nature of the divine demand.

Iqtida refers to a demand issued by the Lawgiver directing the mukallaf toward action or abandonment.

When that demand is jazim, meaning decisive and binding, it produces obligation if it concerns action and prohibition if it concerns abandonment.

When the demand is ghayr jazim, meaning non decisive, it produces recommendation if it concerns action and karaha if it concerns abandonment.

Key Usuli Insight: Strength of Demand, Not the Act

The difference between obligation, recommendation, prohibition, and dislike does not lie in the act itself, but in the strength of the divine demand. The same physical act may carry different rulings depending on whether the talab is jazim or ghayr jazim.

1.4 Talab al Tark

Talab al tark refers specifically to a divine demand to leave an act.

If the demand to abandon is jazim, the ruling is tahrim and the act is haram.

If the demand is ghayr jazim, the ruling is karaha and the act is makruh.

Talab al Tark Is Not Always Prohibition

A demand to abandon an act does not automatically make that act haram. Only when the demand to abandon is decisive does prohibition result. When the demand is non decisive, the ruling is karaha, not tahrim.

1.5 Ibaha and Karaha

Ibaha refers to permissibility. The Lawgiver gives the mukallaf a choice with no praise or blame attached to either acting or refraining.

Karaha refers to a non decisive demand to abandon an act. Leaving the act is rewarded, while performing it carries no sin.

Ibaha Does Not Mean Neutrality of Consequence

Although ibaha grants choice with no inherent praise or blame, an act that is mubah may still become rewarded or sinful based on intention, context, or consequences. Ibaha describes the ruling of the act in itself, not its moral outcome in every circumstance.

1.6 Mahdur and Imtithal

Mahdur refers to that which is legally forbidden and arises from a decisive demand to abandon an act.

Imtithal refers to compliance with the divine command. True imtithal occurs when the act or avoidance conforms to the ruling of the Lawgiver. It applies both to performing obligations and avoiding prohibitions.

Example Clarifying Mahdur and Imtithal

Drinking alcohol is forbidden in Islam and thus falls under mahdur.

If alcohol is presented to a person and they refuse to drink it because they dislike its taste, fear social backlash, or wish to avoid personal harm, then although they have avoided the forbidden act, they are not rewarded for that avoidance. In this case, there is no imtithal, because the avoidance was not done in obedience to the divine command.

However, if the person refuses to drink alcohol out of taqwa and conscious obedience to Allah’s prohibition, then this avoidance constitutes true imtithal. Only in this case is the person rewarded, because their action aligns with the intent to comply with the ruling of the Lawgiver.

Before proceeding, Sheikh Abdullahi, in order to test and solidify our understanding, presented several very interesting case studies. As a disclaimer, it should be noted that making sense of these examples requires a basic foundation in Fiqh, particularly rulings related to taharah, the conditions of salah, and how legal rulings apply within their proper contexts.

1.7 Case Studies

Case Study One: Najasa on Clothing and Dog Licking

In the Shafii madhhab, the saliva of a dog is considered severe impurity.

If a dog licks a person or their clothing, a question arises: is the individual required to purify themselves immediately upon becoming impure, and are they sinful at the moment the impurity occurs?

The answer is that purification is not required immediately. The individual is not sinful merely for being in a state of impurity. What is required is that purification be completed before performing prayer. As long as the impurity is removed prior to salah, there is no sin involved.

Purification becomes obligatory in relation to the act of prayer, not at the moment impurity occurs.

Case Study Two: No Istinja Followed by Wudu and Prayer

If a person answers the call of nature, assuming there is no visible najasa on their body or clothing, and then does not perform istinja, performs wudu, and prays, a question arises regarding their legal state.

In this case, the person is not sinful, and both their wudu and salah are valid. The absence of visible impurity means that the condition of removing najasa has not been violated. While performing istinja is emphasized, the validity of wudu and prayer depends on the presence of actual impurity, not the mere act of using the restroom.

Case Study Three: Sitting Criss Cross in Prayer

Sitting cross legged is not inherently forbidden in the Shafii madhhab.

If a person sits cross legged without excuse in a manner that contradicts proper posture in prayer, the act may be considered makruh. If, however, the posture is adopted due to necessity, discomfort, or ease without violating the integrals of prayer, it is permissible.

In all cases, the prayer remains valid, though the ruling attached to the posture varies based on context and intent.

Sheikh Abdullahi highlighted an important principle concerning intention and reward. This distinction helps clarify when actions or omissions are morally rewarded, beyond their mere legal classification.

Intention and Reward

Wajib and sunnah actions do not require a specific intention for reward. The act itself is rewarded when performed.

For haram and makruh actions, reward is attained only when one intentionally avoids them for the sake of Allah. Mere avoidance without intention does not carry reward.
